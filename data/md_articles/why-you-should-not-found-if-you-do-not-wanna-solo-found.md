# Why You Should Not Found Anything If You Don’t Want to Solo Found

## TLDR

My current view: start alone. Build something real first. Only consider a co-founder once you have traction, clarity, and proof that the company needs a specific person to reach the next stage. At that point, I think you negotiate from strength instead of desperation, and you are less likely to hand over control of the company’s mission or direction too early.

## The Story We All Love

The startup world romanticizes the founding duo. The visionary paired with the operator. The technical brain and the business brain. It makes for clean narratives, strong pitch decks, and sometimes it genuinely works.

But here is my honest take, offered as someone who has not founded anything yet, solo or with others. From the outside looking in, many people seem to treat the search for a co-founder as step one. Before they have validated a single idea, written a line of code, or spoken to one customer, they are already looking for a partner. IMO, that habit deserves a closer look.

## Looking for a Co-Founder Early Is Often About Comfort

When you hunt for a co-founder before anything exists, I think you may be looking for comfort more than capability. You might be outsourcing the hardest part of the journey: full ownership. As a solo founder, at least in theory, you control the variables. You see problems clearly, including the ones happening inside your own head. You fix them fast because there is no one else to wait for. You own the outcome completely, good or bad.

Bring someone in too early and, in my view, the dynamic changes. If something is wrong and it matters to the company, you may not be able to correct it unless that person also sees the problem and chooses to fix it. You can lose speed. You can lose clarity. You can trade ownership for shared responsibility that slowly becomes shared excuses.

## The Real Risk No One Likes to Discuss

For any company you hope will last ten or twenty years, adding another person with real power seems like one of the highest-risk moves you can make. Co-founder breakups are often discussed as one of the common reasons early startups die. People change. Life changes. What felt like a shared mission at twenty-five can look completely different at thirty. Once equity is split and personal lives are entangled, I imagine there is rarely a clean exit.

You can dream of building with your best friend. That feeling is real and beautiful. But I do not think co-founding is the same as working closely with someone you like. It means giving another person executive rights over the direction of the company. That is a different contract entirely.

## Solo Founding Builds the Muscle You Will Actually Need

Founding alone seems uncomfortable. That is also the point, at least from how I currently see it.

It may force a kind of clarity most people never develop. You cannot outsource hard decisions. You cannot blame slow progress on misalignment. You cannot wait for consensus. You move at the speed of your own conviction. I think that discomfort can sharpen you. You see problems faster. You fix them faster. You become the kind of person who can carry real weight.

That growth matters. Your first startup might succeed. It probably will not. Either way, I suspect the person you become while building it solo will be far better equipped for whatever comes next. The next mission, the next team, the next round of capital. All of it seems easier when you have already learned to own everything.

## AI Has Changed the Starting Line

This is also why the current moment feels different to me. AI has made it much easier to begin alone. You can think through ideas faster, build early versions faster, test positioning faster, write outreach faster, analyze customer conversations faster, and keep moving without immediately needing a full team around you.

That does not mean AI replaces judgment, taste, domain expertise, or lived founder experience. It does mean the excuse of "I need a co-founder before I can even start" feels weaker than it used to. A solo founder today can use AI as a thinking partner, coding assistant, research helper, writing editor, sales prep tool, and feedback loop. Many of the early bottlenecks are still hard, but they are less lonely and less blocked than before.

If you want nuance from people who have actually done the thing, seek them out. Talk to experienced founders. Ask operators to review your assumptions. Add advisors if they are genuinely useful. But in my opinion, advice and mentorship are different from handing someone founder-level control before the company has earned that need.

## When a Co-Founder Might Actually Make Sense

There are situations where bringing someone in later can accelerate everything. If you have already worked closely with a person, trust them deeply, and genuinely believe they are the essential piece that lets the company reach the next stage, that conversation is worth having. My bias is: do it after you have built something real. Do it from a position of strength. Do it with clear agreements about roles and decision rights.

Even then, I would expect storms. Co-founding can help you move faster in some areas, but it probably introduces new friction in others. The companies that survive this path seem to do so because both founders entered the relationship with eyes wide open and with real leverage already in place.

## The Conviction Question

Here is what I keep coming back to. If you do not have enough conviction in yourself to start something solo, why should anyone else? Why should a future investor, a future employee, or even a potential co-founder bet on a vision you are not willing to carry alone first?

Looking for a co-founder before you have built anything may signal that you are still waiting for permission or safety. I do not think the market rewards that posture. The best builders I have watched did not wait. They started. They owned the early mess. They figured it out. Only later, when the company had a pulse, did they even consider sharing the steering wheel.

## Start Alone. Build Something Real.

I do not think you need a co-founder to begin. You need conviction, a willingness to be uncomfortable, and the discipline to keep moving when no one is watching. Once you have that, the right people may appear at the right time. And you will be in a position to choose them instead of needing them.

Build first. Own it completely. Then decide who, if anyone, joins the ride.
